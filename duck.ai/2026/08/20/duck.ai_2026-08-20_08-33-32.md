That maps onto LLMs in several ways.

An LLM does not encounter the world directly in the human sense. It processes tokens—words, symbols, and patterns—and builds highly sophisticated abstractions from them. Its “knowledge” is therefore a model of descriptions produced by people, not firsthand access to the things those descriptions concern.

This creates both its power and its limitations:

- **Power:** Abstraction lets an LLM connect ideas, summarize, translate, reason, and generalize across enormous amounts of text.
- **Risk:** The model can produce language that is coherent at the level of patterns while being false or disconnected from reality.
- **Grounding problem:** A sentence may be linguistically appropriate without corresponding to anything actually observed, measured, or verified.
- **Reification:** Users may mistake the model’s fluent concepts for understanding, intention, or direct knowledge of the world.
- **Human responsibility:** People must connect the model’s abstractions back to evidence, context, lived experience, and consequences.

In short, the passage offers a warning about **confusing representation with reality**. An LLM is an extraordinarily capable machine for manipulating representations. It becomes reliable only when those representations are checked against the world—through sources, tools, experiments, observation, and human judgment.
