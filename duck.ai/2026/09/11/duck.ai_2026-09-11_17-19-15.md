What is Baader–Meinhof phenomenon?

Gemma 4 31B:
The Baader–Meinhof phenomenon, also known as frequency illusion, is a cognitive bias where something you recently learned or noticed for the first time suddenly seems to appear everywhere. It is not that the thing has actually become more common, but rather that your brain is now subconsciously scanning for it.

This happens due to two simultaneous psychological processes. The first is selective attention: when you encounter a new piece of information, your brain flags it as important, making you more likely to notice it in your environment. The second is confirmation bias: each new sighting reinforces your belief that the item has suddenly surged in popularity or frequency, leading you to ignore all the times you didn't see it in the past.

Baudelaire had a complex and largely adversarial relationship with photography. While he recognized its technical utility, he viewed it as a threat to the "true" role of art.

In his 1859 critique of the *Salon*, he argued that photography should remain a humble servant to the sciences and arts—useful for archiving historical records, documenting monuments, or providing a base for painters to work from. He believed that as long as it stayed in the realm of the "industrial," it was beneficial.

However, he vehemently opposed the idea of photography as an independent art form. His primary objections were:

**The Lack of Imagination**
For Baudelaire, art was about the "imaginary"—the ability of the artist to interpret, distort, and infuse a subject with emotion and spirit. He saw photography as a mere mechanical reproduction of nature. He feared that if photography were accepted as art, it would encourage a lazy obsession with realism and "the factual," stifling the human imagination.

**The "Slave" to Nature**
He believed that by perfectly mimicking reality, photography trapped the viewer in the physical world. To him, the goal of art was to transcend the visible to reach the invisible. A photograph, in his view, could only capture the surface of a thing, never its soul or its poetic essence.

Despite these critiques, Baudelaire’s obsession with the "modern" and the ephemeral—the very things photography is best at capturing—created a fascinating irony. He wanted to capture the "fugitive" beauty of the city, yet he rejected the one tool capable of freezing that moment in time.
