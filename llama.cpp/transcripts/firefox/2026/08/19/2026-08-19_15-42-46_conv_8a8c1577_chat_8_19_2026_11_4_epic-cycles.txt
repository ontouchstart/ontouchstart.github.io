In the context of software engineering and systems design (which is likely the context of the "Bun 1.4" discussion), an epicycle is a workaround for a fundamental flaw in a system's design.

Instead of fixing the underlying problem, developers add a "layer" on top of it to make it behave correctly. Over time, these layers stack up, making the system incredibly complex and difficult to understand.
The Simple Breakdown:

    The Problem: The original design is slightly "wrong" or doesn't quite work as intended.
    The Workaround: Instead of redesigning the base, you add a new rule or piece of code to correct the behavior.
    The Result: Now you have a "fix" for the "fix," which eventually leads to a messy, tangled web of logic.

Where the name comes from (History)

The term comes from ancient astronomy. To explain why the planets in the sky moved the way they did, ancient astronomers used a "Geocentric" model (Earth is the center of the universe).

Because this model was technically incorrect, they had to imagine planets moving in small circles (epicycles) while orbiting the Earth in a large circle. It "worked" for a while to predict where planets would be, but it was a messy, complicated way to explain a simple truth (that Earth isn't the center).
Software Examples
1. The "Patch" Example

Imagine you build a login system that only allows 5 characters.

    The Problem: Users want longer passwords.
    The Epicycle: Instead of changing the database to allow more characters (fixing the base), you write a piece of code that "compresses" the long password into 5 characters, then "decompresses" it when it's read.
    Why it’s an epicycle: You didn't fix the 5-character limit; you just built a complicated "trick" to bypass it.

2. The "Compatibility" Example (Common in Rewrite Discussions)

Imagine a company is rewriting an old app in a new language.

    The Problem: The new language handles dates differently than the old one.
    The Epicycle: Instead of updating the database to the new format, they write a "translation layer" that converts every single piece of data back and forth every time it is touched.
    Why it’s an epicycle: If they just fixed the data format once, they wouldn't need the translation layer. The layer is an "epicycle" added to keep the old, broken logic alive.

Summary

When people say a project is "full of epicycles," they mean it is becoming a mess of "fixes for fixes" because the core foundation is built poorly.
