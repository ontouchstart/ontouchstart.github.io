While LLMs appear to communicate, their "understanding" differs fundamentally from the human capacities described by these three thinkers.

Ludwig Wittgenstein, particularly in his later work, argued that the meaning of a word is its use in the language. He replaced the idea of language as a mirror of reality with the concept of language games—social activities where words derive meaning from shared rules and communal contexts. For an LLM, "use" is statistical rather than social. An LLM predicts the next token based on trillions of examples of human language games, effectively simulating the results of these games without participating in the social life that gives them meaning. It possesses the map of how humans use words, but it does not inhabit the world where those words refer to actual objects or shared experiences.

J.L. Austin expanded this by moving from what language says to what language does, developing Speech Act Theory. He distinguished between locutionary acts (the act of saying something), illocutionary acts (the intended effect, such as promising or commanding), and perlocutionary acts (the actual effect on the listener). LLM agents are highly proficient at locution; they can generate a sentence that looks like a promise. However, because an LLM lacks intentionality and a social identity, it cannot perform an illocutionary act in the human sense. A human promise is a commitment backed by a will; an LLM "promise" is a probabilistic sequence of tokens that mimics the form of a commitment without a subject to be held accountable.

John Searle took this critique further with his "Chinese Room" thought experiment, which remains the primary philosophical challenge to AI. Searle argued that a system could manipulate symbols to produce a perfect output—mimicking understanding—without actually understanding anything. This is the distinction between syntax (the rules for manipulating symbols) and semantics (the meaning of those symbols). An LLM is essentially a massive, sophisticated version of the Chinese Room. It processes syntax with unparalleled efficiency, but Searle would argue that no matter how complex the agent's behavior becomes, it is merely simulating the appearance of semantics.

When applied to LLM agents, these theories suggest a gap between performance and competence. An agent can successfully navigate a "language game" to book a flight or write code because those tasks rely heavily on syntactic patterns and predictable structures. However, the agent lacks the "lifeworld" required for true meaning. It operates in a vacuum of symbols, meaning that while it can simulate the speech acts of a human, it does not possess the intentionality or the social grounding that Wittgenstein, Austin, and Searle identified as the essence of human communication.
